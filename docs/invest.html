Cash machines: free cash flow against market value

Companies whose free cash flow, after stock-based pay, is at least 10% of their enterprise value (market value plus net debt: what buying the whole company costs, its debts included), from all US-listed stocks. Measured against market value alone, an indebted company's cash flow looks far bigger than what shareholders get, since lenders are paid first. Stock handed to staff is subtracted too: it costs owners through dilution even though no cash leaves the company. ZIM in 2021 was the extreme case: a year of cash flow close to the price of the entire company. It was also the top of a shipping boom, and its cash flow collapsed in 2023. So the verdict asks whether the cash is durable (high in every one of the last four years) or a peak (one year far above the rest). "4 yrs of FCF vs price" above 1.0× means the last four years of cash flow exceed what the market charges for the whole company today. How this works

  • Every S&P 500 company gets four scores, as percentiles against the rest (100 is best): quality (return on capital, cash margins, steady margins, low debt), growth (revenue, earnings and cash flow over three years, plus analysts' next year), dividend growth (yield, five-year growth, years of raises, how well cash flow covers it) and value (cash-flow yield, price against its own history, expected return). Overall leans on quality: 45%, then 20% each growth and value and 15% dividend.
  • The shortlist is the best overall among companies scoring 60+ on quality, with positive cash flow and net debt under 3.5× operating cash earnings, at most four per sector.
  • Buy area: the nearest long-term support at or below the price (40-week average, one-year anchored VWAP, one-year volume value area, 52-week low) down to the next support at least 4% below it. It is then checked against the company's usual valuation.
  • Free cash flow is always counted after stock-based pay, which dilutes owners like any other cost.
  • Targets: free cash flow per share (earnings for banks, insurers and real estate) grown at the company's own recent rate, capped at 20%. Base (1 year) assumes the valuation multiple closes half the gap to its usual level; stretch (3 years) all of it.
